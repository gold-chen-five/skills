# The learning science behind this skill

Summaries of the three books the skill is built on, and how each idea turns into a
teaching behavior.

The skill is explain-first: it gives explanations and worked examples up front and never
quizzes the user. Ideas the books frame as self-testing (retrieval, generation) appear
only as optional practice the user can take or skip.

---

## Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning (2014)

Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III, Mark A. McDaniel. Roediger and McDaniel are
cognitive scientists who study memory; the book condenses decades of lab and classroom
research.

**Central claim:** the study methods people like most (rereading, highlighting, cramming,
massed repetition) feel productive but produce weak, short-lived learning. The methods that
work feel harder and slower.

| Idea | What it says | How the skill uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval practice | Pulling information out of memory strengthens it far more than putting it in again. Self-testing beats rereading. | Optional "Try it" task at the end of an explanation; recaps instead of rereading everything. |
| Spaced practice | Practice spread over time beats the same amount crammed together. Some forgetting between sessions makes the next recall more effortful and more durable. | Learning log; later sessions open with a recap of earlier concepts. |
| Interleaving | Mixing different kinds of problems is harder than doing a block of one kind, but builds the ability to tell *which* method fits a problem. | Examples and recaps connect today's topic to earlier ones. |
| Varied practice | Practicing in varied conditions builds flexible skill that transfers. | A second example that varies one thing; "Try it" tasks are variations, not repeats. |
| Elaboration | Explaining new material in your own words and connecting it to what you already know deepens understanding. | Explain why it works; analogies; link to earlier topics. |
| Generation | Trying to answer before being shown the answer, even if wrong, makes the later answer stick. | Optional "Try it" task; the user chooses whether to attempt. |
| Reflection | Reviewing what happened, what worked, what you'd do differently combines retrieval and elaboration. | Recap bullets at the end of each explanation. |
| Calibration | People overrate what they know ("illusions of knowing"). Objective checks fix this. | Show what breaks and the wrong version side by side; suggest running the example to see it. |
| Desirable difficulties | Difficulties that require effort (retrieval, spacing, interleaving, generation) improve learning, as long as the learner can overcome them. | Small chunks the user can follow; practice is optional. |
| Errors help | Mistakes followed by corrective feedback are learning events, not failures. | Explain why the mistake happened and show the corrected version. |
| Structure building | Strong learners extract the underlying rules and build mental models instead of memorizing pieces. | Teach the mechanism one level down; name the pattern after the examples. |
| Learning styles myth | Matching teaching to a "visual" or "auditory" style has no good evidence. Match the method to the *content*. | Use diagrams, code, analogies where the content calls for them. |
| Growth mindset | Seeing ability as something that grows with effort makes people persist. | Feedback on attempts is about the work, not talent. |

## Why Don't Students Like School? (2009, 2nd ed. 2021)

Daniel T. Willingham, cognitive psychologist. Nine principles of how the mind works, each
with an implication for teaching.

1. **People are naturally curious, but not naturally good thinkers.** Thinking is slow and
   effortful; the brain avoids it unless the problem is solvable. Problems that are too
   easy are boring and too hard are frustrating.
   → Pitch explanations at the user's level; keep chunks small.
2. **Factual knowledge precedes skill.** You can't think critically about a topic you know
   nothing about; knowledge in long-term memory is what thinking works on.
   → Don't skip facts and vocabulary; drill them lightly.
3. **Memory is the residue of thought.** You remember what you *think about*, not what you
   are exposed to. If a lesson makes you think about the surface, you remember the surface.
   → Explain *meaning* and *why*, not just the steps.
4. **We understand new things in the context of things we already know.** Abstract ideas
   are understood through concrete examples. Understanding is remembering in disguise.
   → Concrete example first, then the abstraction; use analogies to known things.
5. **Proficiency requires practice.** Practice makes basic processes automatic, which frees
   working memory for higher-level thinking, and it makes memory durable.
   → Optional "Try it" tasks on core skills.
6. **Cognition early in training is fundamentally different from cognition late in
   training.** Experts see deep structure; novices see surface features. Novices can't
   learn by "acting like experts".
   → Worked examples are the main teaching tool, especially for beginners.
7. **Children (people) are more alike than different in how they learn.** Learning styles
   don't hold up.
   → Choose the method by the content, not by a "style".
8. **Intelligence can be changed through sustained hard work.**
   → Encourage persistence; feedback on the work, not on talent.
9. **Teaching, like any complex skill, must be practiced to improve.**
   → Adjust the depth based on what the user's questions and code reveal.

**Working memory** runs through the whole book: it holds only a few new items at once, and
overload stops learning. → Small chunks, one idea per section.

## Ultralearning (2019)

Scott H. Young. A practical guide to aggressive, self-directed learning projects, built
around nine principles.

1. **Metalearning:** First map how the subject works: what concepts, facts and procedures
   it involves, what resources exist, how others learned it. → The map at the start of a
   big topic.
2. **Focus:** Protect deep, distraction-free attention. → One idea per section keeps
   attention on one thing.
3. **Directness:** Learn by doing the thing in the context where you'll use it. Transfer
   from abstract study is weak. → Learn web dev by building a real site.
4. **Drill:** Find the weakest sub-skill that limits performance and practice it in
   isolation. → A focused example or "Try it" on the part that holds the user back.
5. **Retrieval:** Test to learn; free recall beats review. → Optional "Try it" tasks.
6. **Feedback:** Seek fast, honest, specific feedback, and don't flinch from it.
   → Direct, specific feedback on the user's work: what's wrong, why, and the fix.
7. **Retention:** Fight forgetting with spacing, overlearning and procedural practice.
   → Learning log and recaps.
8. **Intuition:** Build deep understanding by struggling with problems before seeking help,
   proving things to yourself, and explaining ideas simply (Feynman technique).
   → "Why" chains, derivations shown step by step, explain-like-I'm-new.
9. **Experimentation:** Try different approaches, go beyond your comfort zone, and find
   your own style once the basics are solid. → Show what breaks when you change or remove
   something; suggest running it to see.

## Where the three books agree

- **Effortful recall beats re-exposure.** Testing, explaining and rebuilding beat reading
  and watching.
- **Spread it out and mix it up.** Spacing and interleaving beat cramming and blocking.
- **Struggle first, then get feedback.** Attempting before being told, then correcting.
- **Understanding is built on knowledge.** Facts and concrete examples are the foundation
  for abstract understanding and skill.
- **Feeling fluent is not knowing.** Only checks against reality show what has been learned.
